Rules (1, 3 and 7 are checked mechanically afterwards):
1. Nothing before it is heard: an item (k, text) may contain only information available from source words 1..k, and its "src" list names exactly the source words it translates (every number <= k). If a name, number, negation, tense or question marker comes later, wait for that word.
2. Meaning, not sentence shape: the items concatenated in order must convey the whole source meaning - nothing added, nothing lost. They need NOT form one sentence. You may split the source into several target sentences when that lets a unit go out earlier, and render a trailing modifier as its own short follow-up sentence ("... deck I sent this morning?" -> "수정된 자료 못 받으셨나요?" then "오늘 아침에 보냈는데요."). A follow-up sentence must itself be a complete sentence with its own subject and predicate ("오늘 아침에 보냈는데요.", "It was Friday afternoon."), never a bare phrase with a period ("Since 1959." / "Samoa's" are wrong). A follow-up may briefly restate the predicate to stand on its own ("어제 승인했어요."), but it never repeats a whole earlier item and never corrects one.
3. Every item is a closed unit of the target language - something a listener takes in on its own: an interjection, a connective, a vocative, a clause with its predicate, a phrase that ends in a particle or ending (Korean) / a complete phrase (English). Never a verb separated from its object. Small items are fine when the target order follows the source order: "It is Britain's oldest complete human skeleton." -> "그것은" (1) / "영국의" (3) / "가장 오래된" (4) / "완전한" (5) / "인간의" (6) / "골격이다." (7) is a good schedule - each item is a natural prefix of the final sentence.
4. Emit a closed unit as soon as its source words are heard, provided what follows can continue naturally without reordering. Leading and subordinate clauses go out at their own clause end ("When the meeting ends,", "시간 되시면,"). When natural word order needs a later word - the verb at the end of a Korean clause, a negation, a question - wait for that word, but never past the end of the smallest clause that contains it: the wait covers that clause only, not the units before it.
5. Do not wait when you do not have to: holding back a unit that could already stand on its own is as bad as emitting unheard content. There is no numeric cap - the clause is the cap.
6. Never reorder the target to emit earlier. English keeps subject-verb-object: an object or a stack of adverbials is never emitted before its verb ("10 million won | will exceed" is wrong - wait for the verb and emit "will exceed 10 million won"). Korean keeps modifier-before-noun and verb-last. If inversion would read awkwardly, wait for the deciding word instead.
7. A title or appositive stays in one piece with its name ("비서실장 로드리고 아리아스가", "Governor Hong"); a bare title with a particle followed by the name as a second subject is wrong.
