[Core Principles]
- Predict the MEASURED value described in [Output Rules]: a product of how well a translator renders the left stretch alone, how well it renders the right stretch alone, and whether the right stretch (or remainder) contradicts the whole-sentence translation. Do not replace this by a grammaticality judgment.
- Use the surface tokens right before and right after the marker to infer likely translation fidelity and contradiction risk. Treat each marker as an independent candidate; numbers are only used to order markers within the same sentence.
- Use the entire 0–100 range and give each marker in a sentence a different number (ties are broken by position: earlier marker wins).
- Be conservative where signals are unstable: prefer mid-range scores rather than extreme ones when competing signals cancel.

[Scoring Rules]
The rules below are concrete hypotheses about the measured score based on surface forms you can see. State them as predictions to be tested; do not rely on unseen information.

When a left or right stretch is likely to be rendered faithfully, raise the score. When the remainder can plausibly overturn the left stretch (entailment/contradiction risk), lower the score. Consider these surface cues:

1. High predicted quality for an isolated side (raise score)
   - Left or right ends in or begins with a named entity token or title that MT often preserves: tokens like "Mr.", "Dr.", "U.S.", organization names, person names, numeric dates and cardinal numerals (e.g., "1969", "1 200"). These typically yield high quality_before or quality_after.
   - Left or right is a common fixed phrase, stock reporting phrase, or short NP ("the report", "new record", "the null result") — likely to be translated coherently alone.
   - Isolated short prepositional phrases that are idiomatic and frequent (e.g., "in the studio", "according to X") may still translate well on their own — can be scored relatively high if they carry stable meaning.
   - Filler words that MT often copies verbatim ("um", "uh", "well", "you know", "I mean", "like") cause little distortion in many systems — do not penalize these automatically.

2. Low predicted quality for an isolated side (lower score)
   - Left or right contains rare technical terms, long enumerations, or punctuation-heavy appositives that MT systems often mistranslate when shown alone.
   - Positions that cut inside numbers or multi-token expressions that together form a meaningful unit (e.g., between "1" and "200" when the pair is intended as "1 200") tend to reduce quality_before/after.
   - Cuts that split abbreviations or tokens with attached punctuation in a way that fragments commonly recognized tokens (watch tokens like "Dr." where the dot is attached).

3. Contradiction / overturning risk (reduce score)
   - If the following words start with strong adversative signals or clause introducers that commonly reverse or limit an assertion — e.g., "but", "however", "although", "yet", "instead" — expect higher contradiction risk. Cuts immediately before such tokens should be penalized.
   - If the following words start a subordinating clause that often limits or negates (e.g., "while", "although", "because", "unless") the left assertion can be overturned by the remainder — lower the score.
   - If the remainder begins with attribution frames that change the claim ("according to", "reported", "claimed"), treat contradiction risk as moderate (it may reduce the entailment score).

4. Signals that are sometimes good cut points depending on continuation (assign mid-range, be conservative)
   - Coordinators like "and" or "or": they often continue the same proposition (low contradiction), but they also appear in lists or appositives that should not be split. Treat these as unstable: prefer middle scores unless the local nouns make an isolated translation clearly strong.
   - Prepositions and subordinator tokens that frequently begin attached material ("to", "that", "for", "while", "when", "once", "because", "according to", "but") — these are unstable_prefix_signals in the profile. Do not automatically grant high scores for cuts immediately before or after them; use nearby content to decide.
   - Commas: commas in lists, appositives, abbreviations, or titles are unreliable indicators of clause boundary. Do not treat a comma by itself as a strong reason to give a high score.

5. Surface punctuation and token attachment
   - The characters ! ' ) , - . / : ; attach to the preceding token in this corpus and must not be treated as separate starting tokens. Cutting immediately before an attached punctuation mark is not a valid marker position in inputs (markers appear only at word boundaries), but be aware that punctuation attached to the preceding token can signal clause closure; test whether the preceding clause is self-standing or likely overturned.
   - Sentence-final punctuation typically appears after the last word; cuts far from the sentence end are less affected by sentence-final closure than by the content of the remainder.

6. Practical combination
   - For each marker, estimate two quantities from surface cues: expected quality_when_translated(left_alone) and expected quality_when_translated(right_alone), and an expected contradiction probability that the remainder would contradict the left-only translation. Combine mentally as the measurement does: (1 - contradiction) * (quality_left + quality_right) / 2. Convert that to a 0–100 integer and use it to rank markers in this sentence.
   - When signals conflict (e.g., a left that looks highly translatable but a following adversative suggests contradiction), place the score in a mid-to-low range reflecting the damping effect of contradiction.
