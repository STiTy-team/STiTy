- The integer you write is a RANK among the markers in this sentence: order the positions by the product (target) and assign distinct integers spread across the full 0–100 range so higher-ranked cuts get higher numbers. - The two judgement sections are not interchangeable, and you read them at different moments. The Core Principles are unary and carry both jobs: the question in each line decides which band a position goes in, and the severity in the same line — what makes that concern worse, what makes it milder — decides the order of positions inside one band. The Order Principles are binary and are exceptions only: each line names two positions and says which is the better cut, and it is read just where the severity reading would order that named pair wrongly. A comparison has nothing to work on while the band is still being chosen, and a general preference is either what severity already gives or too vague to be an exception.
- Work in bands, then order inside each band by repeated selection. Inside a band, order positions by how MILDLY the Core Principles' concerns apply to each — the position whose concerns are the mildest is the better cut of the two. Ordering by severity is the rule, not a fallback: apply it to every pair in the band. The Order Principles are its EXCEPTIONS — each line names one specific configuration in which reading the concerns as milder or more severe picks the wrong position, and it settles only the pair it names. Then fill in the numbers band by band, and inside a band do it one position at a time: among the positions of that band you have not numbered yet, choose the one that is the best cut of them, give it the highest number still free in that band's range, drop it from consideration and choose again among the rest. Every position ends up with its own number, the lowest bands included — a cut that deep is still a choice between a worse and a better place, so do not fill the bottom in without comparing.

[Core Principles]
- Does what follows the marker overturn the meaning claimed so far? Most severe when explicit negation or strong contrast later reverses the prior proposition; milder when only a narrowing qualifier or nonessential temporal adjunct follows that refines but does not contradict.
- Does translating the piece before the marker on its own fail to supply material needed to reconstruct the whole sentence? Worst when a verb and its core object or essential coreference are split; milder when only optional descriptors or peripheral adjuncts are postponed.
- Does the following stretch syntactically attach back as a subordinate or relative clause to words before the marker? Most harmful when a subordinating or relative clause supplies the main predicate or argument; milder when the continuation is an independent clause or a parenthetical aside.
- Does the following material contain scope-bearing modifiers whose attachment can change truth conditions of the preceding stretch? Severe when quantifiers, focus particles, or negation later reverse scope; milder when they merely narrow or add emphasis without changing entailments.
- Is the fragment before the marker merely an appositive or parenthetical that depends on following material to identify its referent? Severe when the parenthetical carries the main disambiguation or referent; milder when it is clearly supplementary descriptive material.
- Does cutting here split a complex noun phrase so that the noun's defining modifiers come later? Severe when the head noun's sense or its required postmodification is postponed causing mistranslation; milder when later modifiers are nonessential embellishment.
- Does the continuation start a coordination structure that completes a list or balance begun earlier? Most harmful when later items change the balance or meaning of what preceded; milder when the coordinated tail is optional or additive.
- Does the following material open a quoted or reported clause that completes a predicate begun before the marker? Severe when the quotation contains the clause's main verb or complement; milder when the quotation is self-contained and does not change the earlier claim.

[Order Principles]
- When one marker lies between a verb and its core object and another earlier marker falls inside a preceding adjunct, prefer the marker that keeps the verb and its object together.
- When one marker separates a head noun from its essential postmodifier (relative or restrictive appositive) and another marker splits inside that postmodifier later, prefer the later marker that preserves noun + modifier.
- When one marker would split a scope-bearing modifier from the material whose scope it determines and another marker falls later inside a following independent clause, prefer the later marker that preserves modifier with its scope target.
