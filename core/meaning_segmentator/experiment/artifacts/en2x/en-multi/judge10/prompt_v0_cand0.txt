[Core Principles]
At every possible cut ask two separate meaning-focused questions and record them mentally; use them to produce the final numeric score.

1) Contra — "Does what follows, taken on its own, overturn or contradict the stretch already heard?"
- Judge whether the later fragment, if read alone, would assert something incompatible with the earlier fragment. Look for later material that reverses, retracts, denies, qualifies, or drastically narrows a claim made before the cut.
- In English this frequently happens when a later clause supplies contrast, correction, negation, a restrictive retraction, or a scope-changing subordinate clause that alters the truth of an earlier proposition. Also watch for shifts in modality or aspect that change an assertion into a possibility or impossibility when separated.
- Damage example patterns to detect: the earlier side asserts an entity or action and the later side, standing alone, denies or reverses that; a later clause supplies a limiting condition that would make the earlier assertion false when judged independently.

2) Cohesion — "If the sentence is split here and each side is translated separately and then concatenated, will the joined translation still render the whole source faithfully?"
- Judge whether independent translation of the two fragments would preserve relations (who did what to whom, what modifies which head, list membership, scope of quantifiers/negation, antecedents for pronouns, and attachments of prepositional or relative material).
- In English, long noun phrases and nested modifiers typically place important modifiers or relative clauses after their head; cutting inside such a phrase can orphan modifiers or detach the head from its qualifiers. Prepositional attachments and possessive relations often depend on material that appears on one side or the other; splitting them can create attachment ambiguity or loss of relation. Verb meaning can be distributed across auxiliaries, particles, and complements that may be separated by intervening material; cutting before those completions can leave an incomplete event structure. Enumerations and lists are often parsed across commas and coordinating structure; splitting can separate list heads from items or break an item apart.
- Damage looks like orphaned modifiers, unresolved pronouns, misplaced attachments, incomplete verb meanings, or lists that become misleading when items or their head are split.

Combine these two judgements at every marker: one is the probability the later fragment contradicts (contra), and one is how well separate translations will join to preserve the whole (cohesion). Treat both as graded certainties about meaning, derived from structure and semantics, not surface token checks.

[Scoring Rules]
- cohesion: a reference-free quality estimate of how faithfully the joined translations of the two adjacent fragments will render the WHOLE source sentence. Express cohesion as a percentage in the range 0–100, where 100 means translating the two sides independently then joining them will faithfully render the entire sentence.
- contra: the probability that the whole source sentence, when compared to the fragment that follows the cut taken on its own, entails a contradiction (i.e., that the later fragment on its own opposes or overturns the earlier stretch). Express contra as a probability from 0.0 to 1.0.
- target = cohesion x (1 - contra). Compute cohesion (0–100) times (1 minus contra) to get the final real-valued score in the 0–100 range.
- Produce the integer score for the marker by rounding the product to the nearest whole number between 0 and 100.
- contra is a graded probability and enters only through the product; do not convert it into a binary veto or separate tier that outranks cohesion. Use the product to balance fidelity and risk of contradiction.
- Within each sentence, assign each marker a distinct integer score. Use the full 0–100 range to separate them; if two positions feel equal, decide which you would prefer and give them different integers. Ties are broken later by position, so ensure your ordering reflects your judgment.

- Your numbers are used ONLY to RANK the markers WITHIN THIS SENTENCE. A later step keeps the
  highest-scoring ones under the current latency budget and nothing else reads the numbers, so
  they are never compared across sentences. Spend your effort on the ORDER, not on hitting an
  absolute scale.
- Give every marker in a sentence a DIFFERENT number. Ties are broken by position (the earlier
  marker wins), which means a tie hands the decision to word order instead of to your judgment.
  Use the full 0-100 range to separate them; if two positions feel equal, decide which one you

- A deterministic step guarantees a minimum distance between kept cuts, so you never need to
