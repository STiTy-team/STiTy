[Core Principles]
- Predict measured signals, not grammatical intuitions. The measurement came from (a) translating the left stretch alone, (b) translating the right stretch alone, and (c) testing whether the left-translation contradicts the whole-sentence translation. Use only surface cues in the source text that correlate with those facts.
- Anchor judgments to concrete surface forms and short word sequences visible around each marker: the word immediately before the marker, the word immediately after, punctuation attached to the preceding token, and small fixed phrases (e.g. "and", "but", "that", "which", "who", "when", "after", "since", "while", "as", "due to", "by which", "the", "a", "an", "to", "in", "at", "by", "for", and fillers like "um", "uh", "well").
- Treat coordinating connectors and subordinators as signals that often change translation behavior, but state them as testable predictions: e.g. a marker immediately after "and" or "but" can score high in practice if translators render the left fragment well; a marker immediately after "that" or "which" can score low or high depending on how those words serve as clause boundary signals in the surface context.
- Avoid rules that depend on unseen syntax or on any target language. Do not claim that syntactic completeness is what raises a score — the measurement does not look at grammar. Instead name observable tokens and local punctuation patterns and predict how they tend to correlate with the measured quantity.
- Use the full 0–100 range within and across sentences so that markers are well-separated; within each sentence, give every marker a different number.

[Scoring Rules]
The following are concrete, testable surface-based heuristics. Phrase them as predictions to be tested on the corpus; they are not absolute truths about "good" cuts but are intended to reproduce measured outcomes.

- Subordinators / relative pronouns:
  - If the word immediately after a marker is a subordinator or clause-introducing relative (surface forms: that, which, who, when, after, since, although, while, as, because, due to, by which) then the right stretch often carries the subordinate complement needed to complete the clause. Prediction: cuts that leave the subordinate word on the right tend to score higher than cuts that leave it on the left (because a translator gets the subordinate complement intact when it appears on its own). Conversely, markers that end the left stretch with one of these subordinators (left ends with "that", "which", "who", etc.) are often lower-scoring because the left fragment lacks the complement the translator needs.
- Coordinators and discourse connectors:
  - If the word immediately after a marker is a coordinator or discourse connector (and, but, however) and it starts an independent clause (often signalled by a preceding comma or clause boundary), prediction: the marker may score high because both sides can be rendered independently. If the coordinator connects nouns or inside an NP (e.g. "X and Y" as a noun coordination), prediction: the marker will often score low because it splits a single noun phrase that translators typically treat as one unit.
- Determiners and articles:
  - If the left word is a determiner or an unstable prefix signal (the, a, an, this, that when used as demonstratives) then prediction: cutting immediately after that determiner usually scores low because the left fragment lacks the noun that translators need to render.
- Prepositions and of/possession-like tokens:
  - If the left word is a preposition (in, at, on, by, to, for, of) or the word after the marker is a preposition, prediction: markers that separate prepositions from their object tend to score low (translators often need the object to render the phrase faithfully).
- Auxiliary-like tokens and light verbs:
  - If a marker splits an auxiliary or light verb (will, would, have, be, been, is, are) from its main lexical verb (e.g. left ends with "will" and right begins with a verb), prediction: such cuts are often low-scoring because translators rely on the verb phrase together to render tense/aspect/modal meaning well.
- Nouns and clause-final verbs:
  - If the left stretch ends after a full verb phrase or a noun phrase that closes an independent clause (often signalled by a main verb followed by an object, or a comma followed by a coordinator), prediction: such markers often score high because the left side is independently renderable. However, if what follows is a contrasting subordinate clause (however, although, but, a negative clause), measured entailment may reduce the score — watch for contrastive connectors immediately after the marker.
- Fillers and hesitation tokens:
  - If the left or right stretch includes fillers (um, uh, er, ah, well, you know, I mean) they can either lower translation quality scores or have little effect depending on how the model handles them. Prediction: markers adjacent to fillers tend toward mid-to-low scores unless the filler is isolated and the remaining fragment is still rendered faithfully.
- Punctuation and commas:
  - The corpus treats the characters ! % ' ) , - . / : ; as attaching to the preceding token and they should never begin a new segment. Prediction: commas that separate clauses are useful surface cues: a marker immediately after a token that carries a comma often signals a clause boundary and may raise the score if each side is independently translatable. But do not assume every comma marks an independent clause — test relative token patterns (comma + coordinator, comma + relative pronoun).
- Short left or right stretches:
  - Extremely short fragments (one or two tokens) can still score high if translators reliably render them in isolation; conversely long fragments may score low if their isolated translation is poor. Prediction: do not systematically penalize short fragments; rely on surface tokens (auxiliary vs. lexical verb vs. determiner) to decide.
- Contradiction risk signals:
  - If the remainder (the words after the marker) begins with an apparent negator or contrastive connector (not, however, but, although, despite, yet) prediction: the measured entailment term (1 - contradiction) may be lower, so score the marker lower even if both halves translate well separately.
- Non-boundary traps:
  - Beware tokens listed as non-boundary traps in the profile (and when it coordinates nouns; as in light verb patterns like "quoted as saying"; prepositions inside NPs: "bag of leaves"; articles inside NPs). Prediction: markers that cut inside those patterns tend to measure poorly.
- Tie-breaking and distribution:
  - Use the full 0–100 range. Separate nearby positions by different integers so the later deterministic selection can rank them. Treat equal-seeming positions by choosing the one you would prefer to cut and score it higher.

Do not assert any rule that depends on a target language, on unseen syntactic structure, or on "well-formedness." Phrase these as surface-conditioned predictions to be checked against the measured translation-and-entailment values.

[Decision Procedure]
Read the whole sentence once; for each marker judge the left stretch, the right stretch, and what the remainder could overturn; write the number.
