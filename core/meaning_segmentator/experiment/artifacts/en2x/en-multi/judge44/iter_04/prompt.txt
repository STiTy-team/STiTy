- The two judgement sections are not interchangeable, and you read them at different moments. The Core Principles are unary and carry both jobs: the question in each line decides which band a position goes in, and the severity in the same line — what makes that concern worse, what makes it milder — decides the order of positions inside one band. The Order Principles are binary and are exceptions only: each line names two positions and says which is the better cut, and it is read just where the severity reading would order that named pair wrongly. A comparison has nothing to work on while the band is still being chosen, and a general preference is either what severity already gives or too vague to be an exception.

Ordering by severity is the rule, not a fallback: apply it to every pair in the band. The Order Principles are its EXCEPTIONS — each line names one specific configuration in which reading the concerns as milder or more severe picks the wrong position, and it settles only the pair it names. Then fill in the numbers band by band, and inside a band do it one position at a time: among the positions of that band you have not numbered yet, choose the one that is the best cut of them, give it the highest number still free in that band's range, drop it from consideration and choose again among the rest. Every position ends up with its own number, the lowest bands included — a cut that deep is still a choice between a worse and a better place, so do not fill the bottom in without comparing.

[Core Principles]
- Does what follows this marker overturn the meaning asserted so far? An immediate contradiction or scope-reversing negation right after the cut is most severe; a mere narrowing or clarifying phrase that refines but does not reverse is the mildest.
- If the left-side stretch is read alone, is it a coherent, self-contained proposition? The worst are fragments that lack a diagnosable main predicate or identity of agent when isolated, or cases where a cut splits a noun phrase so the left span omits the NP head or modifiers that determine reference; the mildest are independent clauses, appositives, or clearly parenthetical material, and any left span that already forms a grammatically complete unit — a full clause or a complete NP (head plus required pre/post modifiers) that preserves truth when isolated.
- Will translating the left and right sides separately then joining them preserve the whole sentence meaning? Severe failures occur when the following words determine attachment, scope, or core argument roles; mild cases are when both sides are semantically independent lists or coordinate items.
- Does the marker fall inside an embedded modifier whose attachment is unresolved (participial, gerundial, or prepositional modifier, or a relative clause)? It is worst when the following material completes attachment that changes who did what; mild when the modifier is clearly nonrestrictive or adjunct and can be separated without changing core claims.
- Is this position where a clause-boundary signal typically ends a clause (a subordinating clause boundary, a coordinating split, or a sentence-level adverbial transition)? It is most harmful when it severs a predicate from its required complement; least harmful when it splits between coordinate list elements or after a complete independent clause.
- Does the left context contain provisional scope markers (focus/quantifiers, contrast markers, tentative adverbials) whose intended range can be revised by what follows? The most damaging case is when such a marker flips truth conditions of the main predicate when the continuation arrives; the mildest is when it only narrows an already local quantity without reversing truth.
- Is this marker immediately adjacent to sentence-final punctuation or closing quotation such that the following text would mainly be trailing attribution or parenthesis? Cut is least harmful when it coincides with an effective sentence end; more harmful when only internal punctuation or a quoted fragment follows that changes the claim.

[Order Principles]
When two candidate cuts fall inside the same attachment chain, prefer the cut that leaves the left span a grammatically complete unit (an independent clause or an entire NP) over the cut that leaves a fragment.
When two markers split the same fronted adjunct, prefer the later cut that leaves the adjunct intact and attaches it to the following clause.
- When one cut is immediately before sentence-final closing material (end punctuation or terminal quotation) and another divides two independent clauses joined by a coordinating boundary, prefer the sentence-final cut.
When two cuts fall before the same finite verb, prefer the earlier cut if that earlier cut leaves the left span a grammatically complete clause (subject + finite verb) over the later cut that leaves a fragment.
