- The two judgement sections are not interchangeable, and you read them at different moments. The Core Principles are unary and carry both jobs: the question in each line decides which band a position goes in, and the severity in the same line — what makes that concern worse, what makes it milder — decides the order of positions inside one band. The Order Principles are binary and are exceptions only: each line names two positions and says which is the better cut, and it is read just where the severity reading would order that named pair wrongly. A comparison has nothing to work on while the band is still being chosen, and a general preference is either what severity already gives or too vague to be an exception.

Ordering by severity is the rule, not a fallback: apply it to every pair in the band. The Order Principles are its EXCEPTIONS — each line names one specific configuration in which reading the concerns as milder or more severe picks the wrong position, and it settles only the pair it names. Then fill in the numbers band by band, and inside a band do it one position at a time: among the positions of that band you have not numbered yet, choose the one that is the best cut of them, give it the highest number still free in that band's range, drop it from consideration and choose again among the rest. Every position ends up with its own number, the lowest bands included — a cut that deep is still a choice between a worse and a better place, so do not fill the bottom in without comparing.

[Core Principles]
- Does material after the marker overturn the truth or polarity of what came before? Worst when explicit negation or contrastive subordinators later flip the claim; mildest when only a narrowing qualifier or optional modifier follows that refines rather than negates the earlier stretch.
- If the left and right pieces were translated separately, would their joined translation fail to represent the whole sentence? Worst when a verb’s obligatory clausal complement or the main discourse relation is split across the marker; mildest when what follows is an optional adjunct or an incidental modifier.
- Does the marker break a verb’s core predicate-argument connection (verb separated from its object or complement)? Worst when an obligatory object or clausal complement is orphaned on the other side; mildest when a long descriptive NP or adverbial phrase is split from an otherwise self-contained predicate.
- Does the marker separate a subordinating introducer or relative-linking device from the clause it governs, leaving a dependent fragment? Worst when a subordinate clause is left without its main clause attachment or when a relative clause head is absent; mildest when only a nonessential parenthetical or late attachment is postponed.
- Does the cut split participial/gerundial modifiers, appositives, or internal NP structure so the modifier’s target is missing or ambiguous? Worst when a modifier that determines referent or scope is separated from its noun or verb; mildest when the modifier is optional descriptive material whose sense survives apart.
- Does the marker fall inside enumerations or coordination so relations among items are lost? Worst when a coordinating link that determines logical combination or contrast is separated from both joined elements; mildest when list items are independent and the conjunction’s scope is clear from context.
- Does provisional scope (quantifiers, focus words, negation, initial adverbials) on the left get resolved only by later words, so early cutting misrepresents scope? Worst when negation or a contrast marker later applies across the earlier phrase and changes entailments; mildest when the left has a stable quantification or focus unlikely to be overridden.
- Does the marker leave an unfinished quoted, parenthetical, or sentence-final clause whose closure is needed to interpret the prior claim? Worst when an unfinished quotation or closing clause supplies the assertion’s force; mildest when the parenthetical is stylistic and the main assertion already stands.

[Order Principles]
- When one marker sits between a verb and its required clausal complement and another sits earlier inside a preceding adjunct, prefer the marker that keeps the verb and its complement together (preserve verb+complement).
- When one marker divides a relative introducer from the head noun and another falls after the whole relative clause, prefer the later marker that leaves the relative clause intact with its head.
- When one marker falls immediately before the coordinating connector that joins two independent clauses and another falls inside the second clause after the connector, prefer the marker that keeps the connector attached to the clause it governs.
