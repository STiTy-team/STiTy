The Core Principles are unary and carry both jobs: the question in each line decides which band a position goes in, and the severity in the same line — what makes that concern worse, what makes it milder — decides the order of positions inside one band. Ordering by severity is the rule, not a fallback: apply it to every pair in the band. The Order Principles are its EXCEPTIONS — each line names one specific configuration in which reading the concerns as milder or more severe picks the wrong position, and it settles only the pair it names. [Core Principles]
- Does what follows this marker overturn or reverse the truth or entailments of the stretch already heard? An explicit negation or contrastive subordinate that flips truth is most severe; a later narrowing qualifier or harmless elaboration that only restricts scope is milder.
- Would translating the left and right pieces independently fail to preserve core reference or the predicate-argument structure so the recombined translations cannot express the whole sentence? Splitting a finite verb from its subject or essential object is most severe; splitting an optional modifier or adjunct is milder.
- Does the marker fall inside an adjunct or modifier that attaches to a nearby head rather than ending a clause? Breaking participial/gerundial modifiers or appositives from their host NP is worst; separating an optional initial adjunct that can stand alone is milder.
- Does what follows introduce a subordinate or relative clause that depends on the previous noun or clause for attachment and may change attachment or scope? Very severe when the subordinate supplies the main predicate or rescopes negation; milder when it simply adds nonessential descriptive detail.
- Does the marker sit where sentence-level punctuation or an attached character would be stranded or would force the next piece to begin with attached punctuation? This is severe when cutting there would leave attached punctuation orphaned or change clause punctuation; it is milder when punctuation is sentence-final and naturally closes the left segment.
- Is a scope-sensitive word (a quantifier, focus/adverbial, or provisional contrastive particle) immediately before this marker whose interpretation can be overturned by the continuation? Most severe when such a scope item on the left can be reversed by the right-hand clause; milder when no scope-sensitive item precedes.
- Is the left-hand stretch already a self-contained sentence or clause with its main predicate and arguments so it can be understood alone? Best when the left side contains an overt subject and finite verb with complements; milder when it still lacks essential complements or sentence-level adverbials.

[Order Principles]
- When one cut separates an explicit subject from its finite verb and another cut lies later within the same clause after the verb or object, prefer the later cut (after verb) because splitting subject and finite verb breaks predicate-argument cohesion more severely in English SVO.
- When one cut falls between an initial temporal/locative adjunct and the following main clause and another cuts inside the immediately following noun phrase, prefer the cut after the adjunct (keeping the NP intact) because English initial adjuncts are adjuncts, while splitting a noun phrase breaks referential cohesion.
- When one cut would leave a subordinating or relative clause introducer to start the next piece and another cut is placed after that subordinate clause's main predicate, prefer the latter (after the subordinate clause) because opening a piece with an unattached subordinate increases contradiction and attachment ambiguity.
