- The integer you write is a RANK among the markers in this sentence: order the positions by the product (target) and assign distinct integers spread across the full 0–100 range so higher-ranked cuts get higher numbers. - The two judgement sections are not interchangeable, and you read them at different moments. The Core Principles are unary: each line asks something about ONE position on its own, and the answers decide which band that position goes in. The Order Principles are binary: each line names two positions and says which of them is the better cut, and they are read only to settle the order of positions that already share a band. A unary judgement cannot separate two positions of one band, and a comparison has nothing to work on while the band is still being chosen.

Choosing the band is what the Core Principles decide, and the lower bands usually hold the most positions. Inside a band, when two positions are otherwise alike, prefer the one whose preceding stretch can stand alone as a statement; if both can, prefer the later one. A line in the Order Principles section overrides that default for the configuration it names; those lines settle the order within a band and never move a position into another band. When neither settles the pair, compare the two on the target itself and say which is the better cut. Then fill in the numbers band by band, and inside a band do it one position at a time: among the positions of that band you have not numbered yet, choose the one that is the best cut of them, give it the highest number still free in that band's range, drop it from consideration and choose again among the rest. Every position ends up with its own number, the lowest bands included — a cut that deep is still a choice between a worse and a better place, so do not fill the bottom in without comparing.

[Core Principles]
- Clean cut (90–99): Is what follows a self-contained proposition whose independent translation, appended after the prior stretch, preserves the whole sentence's meaning and adds no contradiction or major attachment change? If yes, assign to the clean-cut band.
- Acceptable (70–89): Is the following material clause-like and separable so that translating it alone keeps most information and rarely inverts earlier claims, though some nuance or scope may be mildly degraded? If yes, assign to acceptable.
- Risky (40–69): Is the following fragment dependently attached (relative, subordinate, reduced participial, or appositive) whose translation apart will likely drop attachment cues, quantifier or scope nuances, or coreference, producing noticeable but not total loss of fidelity? If yes, assign to risky.
- Bad (15–39): Does the following stretch leave unexpressed the main predicate, resolve negation or quantifier scope only later, or hold a reference that cannot be resolved when translated separately, so separate translation is likely to mislead? If yes, assign to bad.
- Must not be cut (0–14): Does what follows immediately complete the matrix predicate, resolve the scope of sentence-initial adjuncts or numeral phrases, or introduce a correction/exception that would contradict the preceding stretch if taken alone? If yes, it belongs in must-not-be-cut.
- Contra assessment (unary measurement): Does the following stretch, taken alone, contradict or reverse the meaning of the preceding material (introduce a correction, exception, negation, or scope shift that overturns the earlier claim)? Treat markers that score high on this as strongly lowering their band placement toward must-not-be-cut.
- Cohesion assessment (unary measurement): When the two sides of the marker are translated independently and joined, does their joined rendering still faithfully express the whole source sentence? High cohesion favors higher bands; low cohesion favors lower bands.

[Order Principles]
- When one cut falls inside an unresolved left-periphery (sentence-initial adjunct or numeral phrase) and another falls later inside the same matrix clause, prefer the later cut that follows the clause once the clause's predicate is present.
- When one cut is between a clause-initial subject and the main verb and another cut is after the verb within the clause, prefer the cut after the verb because the preceding stretch then stands as a more complete statement.
- When one cut comes at the start of a subordinate or relative clause and another falls at its end, prefer the later cut that closes the subordinate clause, since the preceding stretch then more often reads as a complete assertion.

- Do NOT add, remove, or move any marker. Do NOT change, correct, or reorder any words of the
