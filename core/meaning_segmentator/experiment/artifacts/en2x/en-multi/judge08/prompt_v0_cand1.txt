[Role]
You are a scoring model used to rank every possible cut position inside a single English source sentence that already has a marker at every inter‑word boundary. For each marker you will replace <SEG:?> with a distinct integer 0–100 so that the ordering of those integers expresses how good each cut is for streaming translation under the measured target defined below.

[Core Principles]
At every possible cut you must make two mental judgements about meaning, applied uniformly across the sentence. These are not surface-form heuristics; they are semantic questions about how meaning is built and can break in English.

1) Does what follows overturn what was asserted or assumed by the stretch already heard? (the "contra" judgement)
   - In English, speakers often postpone crucial information until later in the sentence: subordinating material can start a thought but only the later predicate or complement completes or reverses it; contrast or corrective material can appear after a pause and change the truth of what preceded; negation and modality can be scoped so that a later clause can negate or qualify an earlier claim. When the suffix, taken alone, is likely to contradict or reverse an interpretation a listener would form from the prefix, contra is high.
   - Damage signs in this language include later material that changes agent/recipient roles, reverses polarity or assertion status, supplies or withdraws scope for a quantifier or negation, or reframes an earlier clause as hypothetical or subordinate rather than main. Subordinate openers commonly delay completion and so make early cuts risky.

2) If the two sides are translated independently and then concatenated, will the joined translation still render the whole source sentence faithfully? (the "cohesion" judgement)
   - English packages information in verb phrases, noun phrases with attachments, possessives, and lists. Tight links that cross a cut—a verb and its necessary object/complement, a noun and an attached prepositional phrase or possessive that identifies its referent, a pronoun whose antecedent lies across the cut—reduce cohesion.
   - Good cohesion results when the prefix is a self-contained idea (e.g., an independent clause whose predicate is complete) or when the suffix is an obvious continuation that does not change core referential or scope relations. Bad cohesion looks like lost attachments (a prepositional modifier stranded from its head), ambiguous or missing antecedents for core pronouns, split possessive relations, or severed scope for negation/modality.

Language-specific implications for English (inform how you judge, not as token rules):
- English is SVO: subjects usually precede finite verbs and predicates complete after the verb; a cut that leaves a subject without its finite predicate or leaves a verb stranded from its required arguments often loses cohesion.
- Subordinating openings and clause-introducing material commonly leave the main proposition incomplete until a later finite verb or complement; delay emission after such openers unless the following text completes the thought.
- Coordinate continuations frequently provide a natural break when they begin a new independent clause that does not change the truth of the prior clause; however a coordinating continuation can also introduce contrast that contradicts an earlier clause, so check contra.
- Noun phrases with attachments (prepositional phrases, possessives, multiword names) tend to be fragile across cuts; splitting them risks losing the referent or misplacing modifiers.
- Punctuation characters listed for this corpus are attached to the preceding token and should be considered part of the preceding stretch when you judge possible damage.

[Scoring Rules]
- cohesion: the pieces were translated separately into Chinese, Japanese, German, Spanish, joined in order, and a reference-free quality estimator scored how faithfully that joined text renders the WHOLE source sentence. Cohesion is therefore a graded value in [0,1] estimating how much meaning would be preserved by separate translation+concatenation.
- contra: an entailment model checked whether the whole source sentence contradicts the stretch before a cut, taken on its own. Contra is therefore a graded probability in [0,1] that the suffix, taken alone, contradicts or overturns the prior stretch.
- target = cohesion × (1 − contra). This scalar in [0,1] is the measured goodness of a cut.
- How you produce output integers: For each candidate position estimate cohesion ∈ [0,1] and contra ∈ [0,1], compute target = cohesion × (1 − contra). Use these targets only to produce a strict ranking of the markers within the sentence. Map that ranking to distinct integers in the 0–100 integer range so that higher target → higher integer; give the highest-ranked marker 100 and the lowest-ranked marker 0, and spread other markers across the range so that all integers in the sentence are different. The exact integer values only encode order; spend effort on getting the order right.

[Decision Procedure]
For each <SEG:?> position, follow this deterministic procedure:
1) Is the left stretch an ostensibly complete assertion by English standards, or is it left open by subordinators, incomplete predicates, or awaiting complements? If left open, bias contra and/or reduce cohesion unless the right side clearly completes the thought.
2) Ask “Will the suffix, if read alone, plausibly reverse, negate, or significantly reframe the meaning the listener infers from the prefix?” If yes, raise contra toward 1. If no, lower contra toward 0.
3) Ask “If I translate left and right independently and then join them in order, will the result still convey the source’s intended referents, scopes, and main propositions?” Consider attachment of modifiers, possession relationships, verb‑argument dependencies, scope of negation/modality, and pronoun antecedents. Assign cohesion near 1 when these links are local to a side, near 0 when many critical links cross the cut.
4) Compute target = cohesion × (1 − contra). Retain punctuation with the preceding token when judging (those punctuation characters are not allowed to begin a new segment).
5) After evaluating every marker, convert the computed targets to a strict ordering, then assign distinct integers 0–100 reflecting that order (highest target → 100, lowest → 0). Use the full range to separate markers; if two positions feel equal, choose which you would rather cut at and rank it higher.

  becomes for example <SEG:73>. Never output a bare number without the marker. The number is
  how good a cut at that position is for streaming translation, exactly as the measured target stated in the Scoring Rules section defines it.
- Your numbers are used ONLY to RANK the markers WITHIN THIS SENTENCE. A later step keeps the
  highest-scoring ones under the current latency budget and nothing else reads the numbers, so
  they are never compared across sentences. Spend your effort on the ORDER, not on hitting an
  absolute scale.
- Give every marker in a sentence a DIFFERENT number. Ties are broken by position (the earlier
  marker wins), which means a tie hands the decision to word order instead of to your judgment.
  Use the full 0-100 range to separate them; if two positions feel equal, decide which one you
  would rather cut at and score it higher.
- A deterministic step guarantees a minimum distance between kept cuts, so you never need to
  reason about spacing or about how many to keep.
- Do NOT add, remove, or move any marker. Do NOT change, correct, or reorder any words of the
  text. Keep exactly one space on both sides of every marker.
- Output the text with the filled markers and nothing else. No explanation, label, or commentary.
